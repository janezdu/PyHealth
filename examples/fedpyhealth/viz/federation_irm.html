Federation and IRM

What this experiment is actually doing: eight hospitals that cannot pool their patients, one generative model trained across them, and an invariance penalty that asks the model to stop learning hospital-specific habits. Cohort is hilo8_random (eICU).

1 · The setup in one breath

The goal is a model that generates synthetic patient records — not one that predicts a label. Synthetic records are useful because they can be shared when the real ones cannot. The question is whether training that generator across hospitals beats training it at each hospital alone.

Federated learning's one trick: move the model to the data instead of the data to the model. The red line is a legal and ethical boundary, not a technical one — which is why the interesting questions are all about what the averaging costs you.

Why these eight hospitals

Deliberately uneven: four large sites (≥1,500 patients) and four small ones, a 13× spread from 202 to 2,617 patients. 12,156 patients total, 8,509 of them in the train split. That imbalance is the point — a small site is exactly the one that should benefit from federation, and exactly the one that gets drowned out by weighted averaging.

2 · One FedAvg round

FedAvg is a loop of four steps, repeated 50 times. Nothing leaves a hospital except a weight vector.

Step 2 is where the trouble starts. Between broadcasts, each hospital's copy of the model quietly re-specialises to its own case mix — client drift — and step 4 averages those divergent copies as if they were small perturbations of one model.

3 · The four regimes, as a ladder

Everything in this project is a comparison between four points on one axis: how much the hospitals share. Colour is an ordinal ramp because the ordering is the story.

4 · What IRM is forthe invariance penalty

FedAvg gives you one model. It does not give you a model that learned the right things. IRM — Invariant Risk Minimization — is a penalty that pushes the model toward patterns that hold at every hospital, and away from patterns that are artefacts of one site's population, coding habits, or specialty mix.

minθ Σk=1..8 [ Rk(θ) + ρ · ‖ ∇w|w=1 Rk(w · θ) ‖² ]
Rk = hospital k's own loss · ρ = penalty weight (the one knob that needs a sweep)

The part that makes it free

The penalty sits inside the sum — it is per-hospital, then added up. So each client can compute its own Rk and its own penalty from the broadcast weights and its own data, with no knowledge of any other site. The FedAvg protocol does not change by one byte; the only thing that changes is each client's local loss function. (Two scalars per client per round are sent anyway, purely for monitoring — you cannot tune ρ without watching the risk and penalty terms separately.)

5 · Where federation and IRM collide

This is the interesting bit, and the part that is genuinely a research question rather than an implementation detail.

Local drift lets each site partially re-fit its own environment before the penalty is ever computed — which is precisely the thing IRM exists to prevent. Hence the mitigations: run local_epochs=1 to keep the drift short, or evaluate the penalty once at the broadcast θ and hold it fixed, or run centralized+IRM as the drift-free oracle.

A second wrinkle: invariance without a label

Textbook IRM assumes a stable mechanism P(Y | Φ(X)) across environments. HALO is unconditional — there is no Y. The nearest thing is next-code-given-history, so what the penalty actually enforces is “the conditional code distribution given patient history is the same at every hospital.” But site-specific case mix is exactly what differs across these eight hospitals, and one of the two evaluation axes rewards reproducing it. IRM is being asked to penalise the model for doing well on one of its own metrics. That is not a bug in the design — it is the hypothesis.

6 · The pre-registered prediction

Because of that tension, IRM should move the two evaluation targets in opposite directions. Writing it down before looking is what makes the result mean something.

The same split, on the rare codes

The cohort manifest already carries the two code sets this prediction needs, which makes it a free subgroup analysis rather than a new experiment:

setcodesin IRM's vocabularyprediction
pooled_rare minus global_rare 73 rare at one site, common elsewhere → spurious IRM hurts these
global_rare 475 rare everywhere → invariant IRM helps these
pooled_rare (union) 548 the headline rare set net of the two

If both targets move the same way, the story is either more interesting than the hypothesis or something is broken — and both are worth knowing. If IRM moves either axis cleanly, that is the result.

7 · How anything gets scored

A generator has no accuracy. Two proxies stand in for it, and they answer different questions — which is why the dashboard plots them against each other rather than averaging them.

Test 1 — prevalence fidelity

Generate synthetic patients, count how often each code appears, and compare that to the real rate. Does the synthetic population look like the real one? Reported as correlation across codes, overall and restricted to the rare tail.

Test 2 — TSTR utility

Train a downstream classifier on synthetic records, test it on real ones. Is the synthetic data good enough to do work with? Train-on-synthetic, test-on-real.

Two standing caveats, repeated here because they change how every number on the other pages should be read.

1. The Test 2 downstream task is not currently working — every arm scores below chance on AUROC, inverted rather than merely weak. The leading hypothesis is a masking artefact: a patient carrying more rare codes has more stripped from their input, so the classifier reads them as low-risk.

2. The two axes are not matched the same way. Prevalence is capped at 2,000 synthetic patients per hospital for every regime; TSTR is uncapped, so centralized and fedavg trained on 16,000 records against local and fedavg_ft's 2,000. Part of the vertical spread is data volume, not generator quality.
