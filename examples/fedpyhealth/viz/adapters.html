What does dropping to 0.5% of the parameters actually cost?

Full local fine-tuning retrains all 6.17 million HALO weights per hospital — nearly 44,000 parameters per patient at the smallest site. Three cheaper alternatives freeze the shared trunk and train a small delta instead. All start from the same federated global and see the same data.

The two composed, and the metric decides what you see. Switch this figure between Pearson and R² with the IRM-trunk group on; they tell different stories, and the difference is the finding rather than a contradiction.

On R² the composition lands up and to the right of both parents. IRM + LoRA head reaches 0.630 specialist and 0.732 generalist, against 0.431 / 0.600 for the same adapter on a plain trunk — both axes improve by far more than the 0.079 R² noise floor. The full fine-tune moves furthest of all: 0.058 → 0.569 specialist and −0.172 → 0.039 generalist.

On Pearson the same runs barely move: +0.025 specialist and −0.014 generalist for the LoRA head, both at or inside the 0.019 noise floor. Reporting Pearson alone would have called this a wash.

Pearson is scale-invariant and R² is not, so the split says IRM is fixing absolute prevalence calibration rather than code ranking — which is the failure it was brought in for. The centralized arm on this cohort emitted 25.2 codes per visit against a real 11.5; a generator can rank codes perfectly and still be wrong about how many of them there are, and only R² and RMSE can see it.

Reading it

Parameter count buys nothing among the adapters. LoRA attention (33K), LoRA head (38K) and the last MLP (526K) land within 0.022 of each other on specialist Pearson, across a 16× spread in capacity. If more parameters helped, that gap should not be flat.
The full fine-tune is the better specialist, and the worse generalist. It leads on the site's own fold (0.906 against 0.827–0.849) and trails on the pooled fold (0.950 against 0.970–0.974). More capacity buys sharper personalisation and costs cohort-level fit — the trade-off the adapters were meant to probe, and it is real.
It was steps, and ten times more of them changes the answer. At ft_epochs = 20 the LoRA head reaches 0.903 specialist against the full fine-tune's 0.929 — at 0.61% of the parameters — while holding 0.920 generalist against its 0.900. On rare codes it goes 0.777 → 0.855. That is the trade-off the adapters were meant to buy, and at 2 epochs it was invisible.

LoRA attention still barely moves (0.827 → 0.838), which now reads as a real finding rather than under-training: adapting how the model attends over visit history does not change which codes it emits, and code prevalence is what these metrics score.
Why 2 epochs showed nothing. At ft_epochs = 2 and batch 128, a hospital gets between 4 and 30 optimiser steps — four at the 142-patient site. A LoRA's B is zero-initialised, so an adapter is the trunk until training moves it, and four steps at lr 1e-4 moves it almost nowhere. The evidence is on the first panel: every adapter sits within ±0.02 of the unadapted fedavg point (0.848), while the full fine-tune — which starts from an already-good model rather than from zero — reaches 0.906. The flat response to capacity says the same thing: 16× more parameters changes nothing when none of them have trained. This makes the whole page a measurement of under-training, not of parameter efficiency.
μ did essentially nothing. Turning the proximal penalty on moves specialist Pearson by −0.010 to +0.014 depending on variant, with no consistent sign. The most likely explanation is that μ = 0.01 was picked without calibrating against HALO's loss scale (~4×10⁻⁴), so the penalty is either negligible or swamped. Treat this as untested, not as a null result.

What this does not show

  • Adapters did not rescue the smallest sites. The hypothesis was that a capacity limit would help most where the parameter-to-patient ratio is worst. At hospital 429 (142 patients) the full fine-tune scores 0.774 and the LoRA variants 0.726–0.744. Only the last MLP beats it there, at 0.799 — and that is the largest adapter, which is the opposite of the predicted mechanism.
  • One seed, no intervals. Differences under about 0.02 are not separable from run-to-run noise here, and several of the comparisons above sit at exactly that scale.
  • Rare-code R² is negative for every adapter (−0.05 to −0.40) while the full fine-tune reaches +0.11. On rare codes specifically, the adapters are worse than predicting the mean prevalence — visible only on the R² metric, not on Pearson.
  • The two epoch settings do not share a trunk. fed_rounds = (100 − ft_epochs) / 2, so the 20-epoch runs warm-start from a 40-round FedAvg global and the 2-epoch runs from a 49-round one. Each set is internally matched — its own full fine-tune is in it — but a 2-epoch and a 20-epoch point differ in trunk as well as in fine-tuning length.
  • Learning rate is still untouched. Adapters inherit lr = 1e-4 from the profile; LoRA conventionally wants 10–100× that because it starts from zero. Only steps were varied here, so the ceiling for LoRA head is not yet known.
