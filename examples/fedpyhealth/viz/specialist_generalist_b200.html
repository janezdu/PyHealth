What rare-code upweighting does to those points

--rare-upweight scales each patient’s loss by how rare their codes are at their own site: w = 1 + Σ 1/local_prevalence over that patient’s rare codes, capped at the site’s 99th percentile, normalised to mean 1. Six arms, reweighted, with this panel’s own metric and code-set controls.

Not directly comparable to the panel above. These runs also use train.py’s default early stopping, where every unweighted arm runs --no-early-stop, so they differ in two ways rather than one. centralized shows it most: off-scale −67 rare R² unweighted, −0.42 here.

Test 2 — downstream utility

Can you train a rare-code classifier on this synthetic data?

Test 1 asks whether the synthetic data has the right code prevalences. This asks whether it is useful: an RNN is trained purely on each arm's synthetic patients and scored on real ones (train-on-synthetic, test-on-real). Average precision, because the rare codes sit near 0.3% prevalence — accuracy and AUROC both stay flattering while a model is useless.

Head codes (30) is a different experiment on the same arms: 30 codes drawn at random from the 91 that carry at least 20 test positives, rather than the 478-code tail where half the codes have four positives or fewer. It is the pool on which this test can actually separate arms — but its AP floor is 0.029 against the tail's 0.0067, so read each panel against its own dashed anchors and never across them.

Test 2b — the same arms, scored with logistic regression

The RNN was overfitting — but only where the training set was small

Test 2 trains an RNN on each arm's data. On this pool that RNN scores AUROC 0.417 for real_local — a hospital's own real records, ranking worse than a coin flip. Swapping in one L2 logistic regression per code, on bag-of-codes features, moves that row to 0.644. Nothing about the data changed; the classifier did.

The effect is confined to the starved arms. Every synthetic arm trains on 6,200–7,800 records and the RNN is slightly better than logreg on all of them. real_local is the only arm under budget (142–1,599 records), and across the eight sites the RNN's score tracks training size at r = +0.84. So this is a sample-size failure, not a real-versus-synthetic one.

Read the real_local anchor first. Under the RNN every synthetic arm beats it; under logreg none of them do.

Classifier

RLSP — real local + synthetic pooled

Synthetic data earns its keep when the classifier is starved

Every other Test 2 arm asks whether synthetic replaces real. Nobody deploys that way. RLSP trains one classifier per hospital on that site’s own real records together with the federation’s pooled synthetic set — the question a site would actually ask. Read each bar against the real_local line, which is that site alone.

On the natural mix, FedLIBRA’s synthetic data lifts a site from 0.42 to 0.70, level with FedRep and LG-FedAvg and clearly above FedAvg, Ditto, PerAda and FedProx (0.61–0.62). One oddity: FedRep, LG-FedAvg and FedProx produce synthetic data that scores below chance on its own (0.36–0.47), yet it helps as much as anything once the site’s real records are added. They emit the right co-occurrences at the wrong rates. Only the RNN view is scored for this arm set; real_local sits below chance there because 142–1,599 records overfit it, so read gains against that line, not against 0.5.

The codes scored toggle changes which 30 codes are masked and predicted: a random draw from the well-supported codes (the default), the 30 most common, or 30 rare ones. The ordering holds on all three. FedLIBRA, FedRep and LG-FedAvg form the top group, and FedRep with our mild rate penalty is highest on every code set and mix, by 0.01–0.03 AUROC over FedLIBRA. On downstream utility the rate penalty helps FedRep; on prevalence fidelity it still trails FedLIBRA badly on the generalized axis (rare-code R² −1.22 against +0.61).

Reading this honestly

Which comparisons are clean. The sparse arms and the trunk share one federated global exactly — they were seeded from it and skipped retraining, so differences between them are the adapter and nothing else. The four plain fine-tuning arms each trained their own trunk (--resume was missing), and FedAvg over 40 rounds is not bit-reproducible, so some of the spread among them is trunk draw. The three IRM arms share the IRM trunk with each other.

Pearson and R² disagree, and that is the finding. Switch the dropdown. On R² the sparse ladder spans 0.276–0.478 and IRM + LoRA head reaches 0.633; on Pearson almost everything sits within 0.013 of doing nothing at all. Pearson is scale-invariant and R² is not, so these methods are correcting how much of each code gets emitted, not which codes are relatively common. If ranking codes correctly is what matters to you, none of this has moved.

Two arms are inert. IHT 10% · SGD sits on top of the trunk on every metric — SGD barely moved the weights even at a learning rate 10,000× the Adam default, so it is effectively an un-fine-tuned model wearing an adapter’s name. It is drawn in grey and off by default.

The composition is currently the best arm, and the control undercuts the story. IRM ρ=10² + XM K=4 on fedavg reaches 0.591 / 0.943 on R², beating both of its parents. But dropout 0.3 only — no best-of-K at all — scores 0.496 / 0.923, essentially matching XM K=4's 0.570 / 0.923, and fedavg · IRM ρ=10⁴ reaches 0.525 / 0.932 with neither dropout nor XM. Toggle those on together: most of what looks like a best-of-K effect is reachable without best-of-K.

The best-of-K arms changed the model, so read them apart. They are drawn as squares because they carry dropout 0.3, which nothing else on this page has — so they differ from their baselines in two ways, not one, and the separate dropout-only control has not been scored on this protocol yet. Their R² spread across K (0.412, 0.570, −0.000 for K = 2, 4, 8 on the specialist axis) is thirty times the 0.018 measurement noise floor and is not monotone, which points at run-to-run TRAINING variance rather than an effect of K. That variance has never been measured here: every noise figure on this page comes from scoring one model twice, not from training the same configuration twice.

The trunk is doing more work than the adapter. Sparse fine-tuning on the IRM trunk beats the same adapter on a plain trunk on both axes — generalist R² 0.651 → 0.848 for L1 at λ=0.5, about nine standard errors. And there is no single winner: IRM + LoRA head leads on specialist, IRM + L1 on generalist. Most uncomfortable of all, fedavg · IRM ρ=10⁴ — with no fine-tuning whatsoever — scores 0.525 / 0.932 on R², ahead of every fine-tuned arm on both axes. Toggle the fine-tuning families off and watch where the IRM training points sit.

Full fine-tuning is the cautionary point, not the target. It reaches the best specialist Pearson of any arm and the worst generalist R² of any arm (−0.466) — it fits each site by destroying the shared model, which is the entire case for adapters.
